Video Streaming and CDNs

Slide 1
This slide outlines why video streaming dominates internet traffic and the core networking challenges it introduces.

Massive Bandwidth Dominance:Video streaming platforms (Netflix, YouTube, Amazon Prime Video) account for roughly $80\%$ of all residential ISP network traffic.

Challenge 1 (Scale):How can a central content provider serve billions of concurrent global users without collapsing servers or choking network links?

Challenge 2 (Heterogeneity):End users connect using wildly different devices and network conditions: high-speed wired fiber vs. weak mobile cellular signals, $4\text{K}$ desktop monitors vs. low-resolution smartphones, and fluctuating bandwidth availability.

The Solution:Deploy a distributed, application-level infrastructure using Content Delivery Networks (CDNs) and adaptive streaming protocols to move video content closer to users and adapt dynamically to device capabilities.

 Multimedia — Video Structure & Compression CodingThis slide breaks down what digital video is at a data level and how video compression algorithms cut down file sizes.

Digital Video Basics:A video is simply a sequence of still images (frames) played at a fixed frame rate (eg 24 frames/second).

Frames is a single still photograph in a sequence of images 

Pixel is the smallest individual dot of color and loight that makes up a digital image or display screen 

Each digital image is a 2D grid (array) of pixels, where each pixel is represented by bit values indicating color and brightness.

Video Compression (Coding):Raw uncompressed video is far too massive to stream over the internet, so coders compress it by removing statistical redundancy:

Statistical redundancy is the repetitive or predictable patterns in data where certain values occur far more frequnetly than pure randomness would suggest, 

Spatial Redundancy (Within a Single Image):Adjacent pixels in a frame often have identical colors (e.g., the purple sky at the top of frame $i$).



Temporal Redundancy (Between Consecutive Frames):Adjacent frames in time look nearly identical except for moving objects (e.g., the runner moves slightly between frame $i$ and frame $i+1$, but the background sky remains unchanged).

Compression: Instead of sending a full brand-new image for frame i+1, the encoder only transmits the small difference (motion vector and changed pixels) relative to frame $i$.



Slide 3
This slide explains how video data rates are structured after compression.

CBR (Constant Bit Rate):

The video is compressed to maintain a fixed, unchanging data rate (e.g., always 2 Mbps) throughout the entire video.

Trade-off: Easy to plan network bandwidth, but quality drops during fast-action scenes and bandwidth is wasted during still scenes.

VBR (Variable Bit Rate):

The bit rate dynamically adjusts depending on how much spatial and temporal complexity exists in each scene.

A talking head with a still background needs very few bits, while a rapid action scene with explosions generates much more data.

Common Video Standards:

MPEG-1 (CD-ROM): ~1.5 Mbps (basic quality used in older media).

MPEG-2 (DVD): 3–6 Mbps (standard definition DVD quality).

MPEG-4 / H.264 (Internet standard): Highly flexible, scaling from 64 Kbps (mobile) up to 12 Mbps (HD/4K).

Slide 5

 Streaming Stored Video — Core Network Challenges

This slide illustrates the baseline client-server streaming model (pre-recorded video stored on a server and watched on demand) and its primary obstacles.

The Basic Model:

Video files sit on a remote server, pass through the public Internet, and play on the client's screen.

Challenge 1: Bandwidth Variation Over Time:

Available throughput between server and client is never constant. It fluctuates continuously due to:

Wi-Fi interference within the home.

ISP access link traffic.

Congestion on core Internet routers.

Server load.

Challenge 2: Playback Stalls & Quality Drops:

If network congestion causes packets to drop or arrive late, the client runs out of video data to display, leading to buffering freezes (rebuffering) or degraded video quality.

Slide 7

 Streaming Stored Video — Challenges & Continuous PlayoutThis slide focuses on the strict timing requirements of video playback and why simple transmission fails without a buffer.
 
 Continuous Playout Constraint:Once the user presses play, frames must appear smoothly and continuously at the exact frame rate (e.g., 1 frame every $33.3\text{ ms}$ for $30\text{ fps}$).
 
 If a frame is not present in memory when its exact playback timestamp arrives, the video freezes (rebuffering / buffering wheel)
 
 .The Problem (Network Jitter):Network delays on the Internet are variable (jitter) due to fluctuating congestion.
 
 Because packets arrive in bursts or get delayed unpredictably, the receiver cannot rely on just-in-time delivery.
 
 Solution: The client must build a client-side buffer (a temporary holding queue in RAM) to smooth out network bursts and keep playout continuous.
 
 Additional Practical Challenges:Client Interactivity: Users randomly pause, fast-forward, rewind, or skip ahead, requiring the stream to cancel current chunks and request new offsets.
 
 Packet Loss & Retransmission: Lost packets require retransmission delays, which risk draining the client buffer before the replacement data arrives.

Slide 8

This slide provides the graphical timing breakdown of how a client-side buffer operates to prevent playback freezes.

Constant Bit Rate Transmission (Red Line): The server transmits video bytes into the network at a steady pace.

Variable Network Delay (Jitter): Because internet delays fluctuate unpredictably, the rate at which the client receives bytes fluctuates (the black/wavy arrival pattern).

Client Playout Delay (Horizontal Blue Arrow at Bottom): The intentional delay between when the first byte arrives and when the media player actually starts rendering video on the screen.

Buffered Video (Vertical Blue Arrow): The height difference between the received data and the played-out data represents the reserve cushion of video sitting safely in device RAM

.Core Takeaway: By delaying playout slightly at the start, the buffer builds a reserve large enough that variable network delay (jitter) never causes the playout curve to overtake the reception curve (which would freeze the screen).

Slide 9:

 Streaming Multimedia — DASH (Dynamic, Adaptive Streaming over HTTP)This slide introduces DASH, the industry standard protocol used by Netflix, YouTube, and Amazon to deliver video smoothly over varying network conditions.
 
 Server Responsibilities:Chunking: Slices the full video file into small temporal segments (typically $2\text{–}10\text{ seconds}$ chunks).
 
 Multi-Rate Encoding: Encodes each chunk at multiple distinct quality levels and bitrates (e.g., $360\text{p at } 500\text{ Kbps}$, $720\text{p at } 2\text{ Mbps}$, $4\text{K at } 15\text{ Mbps}$).
 
 CDN Replication: Replicates these multi-quality chunk files across distributed CDN edge servers worldwide.
 
 Manifest File (MPD): Generates a master index file listing URLs, bitrates, and chunk numbers for each quality level.
 
 Client Responsibilities (Client-Driven Intelligence):Bandwidth Probing: Constantly measures real-time download speed and client buffer fullness as each chunk arrives.
 
 Adaptive Requesting: Uses the manifest file to request chunks one at a time via standard HTTP GET requests:
 
 High Bandwidth / Buffer Full: Asks for the next chunk in $4\text{K}$ or $1080\text{p}$
 
 .Bandwidth Drops / Buffer Draining: Seamlessly downgrades and requests the next chunk in $480\text{p}$ or $720\text{p}$ to avoid buffering freezes.
 
 Server Switching: Dynamically requests chunks from alternative or geographically closer CDN nodes if the current server slows down.

Slide 10 

This slide focuses on where the decision-making happens in DASH. The server remains a passive file store, while the client's video player runs the decision logic:

"When" to Request a Chunk:The client monitors its RAM buffer continuously.

It paces requests so that the buffer neither runs completely dry (buffer starvation / freeze) nor overfills and wastes device memory (buffer overflow).

"What" Encoding Rate to Request:The client measures real-time network throughput for each downloaded segment.

If bandwidth is abundant, it requests higher bitrates ($1080\text{p} / 4\text{K}$). If bandwidth drops, it instantly steps down ($720\text{p} \to 480\text{p}$) so the video keeps playing without pausing.

"Where" to Request the Chunk:Using URLs provided in the manifest file, the client can select the CDN edge server that is geographically nearest or exhibiting the lowest network latency.

The Full Streaming Formula:$$\text{Streaming Video} = \text{Video Encoding (compression)} + \text{DASH (adaptive logic)} + \text{Playout Buffering (safety cushion)}$$

Content Distribution Networks (CDNs) — The Scaling DilemmaThis slide sets up the core infrastructure problem of delivering massive video libraries to a global audience.

The Challenge: How to deliver high-bandwidth video from a catalog of millions of titles to millions of simultaneous users across the world without collapsing.

Option 1: The Centralized "Mega-Server" Model (Why it fails):

Single Point of Failure: If the central data center goes offline or experiences a power/fiber outage, the entire service goes down globally

.Network Congestion at the Source: Concentrating millions of simultaneous video streams onto a single facility's outgoing links creates massive bottlenecks.

Long Distance & High Delay: Distant users across oceans experience high RTTs, packet loss across multiple transit ISPs, and poor throughput.

Verdict (Red text at the bottom): "...quite simply: this solution doesn't scale." A centralized architecture cannot support internet-scale video streaming, setting up the need for distributed CDN edge deployments.

Slide 12 

This slide explains the practical solution to the scaling dilemma (Option 2: Deploying a CDN) and contrasts the two main design philosophies used to build CDNs:

The CDN Core Concept:Instead of serving everyone from a single central mega-server, store and serve replicated copies of video content from thousands of servers geographically distributed across the globe.

Strategy 1: "Enter Deep" (Pioneered by Akamai)

Philosophy: Deploy server clusters deep inside local access ISPs and residential networks all over the world

.Advantage: Places content physically and topologically as close to end-users as possible, cutting latency, eliminating transit link congestion, and increasing throughput.

Trade-off: High operational and maintenance complexity to manage hundreds of thousands of individual server clusters deployed in thousands of remote ISP locations

.Strategy 2: "Bring Home" (Used by Limelight / Fastly):Philosophy: Deploy a smaller number (tens or hundreds) of much larger server clusters located in key Points of Presence (POPs) connected directly to major Internet Exchange Points (IXPs).

Advantage: Simpler to maintain, manage, and configure because hardware is concentrated in fewer, larger facilities.

Trade-off: Content sits slightly further from the end user compared to "Enter Deep", traversing more intermediate network hops.

 Slide 14-

 This slide details the concrete end-to-end workflow Netflix uses to deliver video to users worldwide:

Netflix Open Connect CDN:

Netflix operates its own custom CDN called Open Connect.

Copies of popular content (e.g., the show Mad Men) are pre-replicated across thousands of storage appliances placed directly inside local ISPs worldwide.

The Request & Streaming Workflow:

Manifest Retrieval: When a subscriber clicks a title, the client application first queries Netflix control servers in the cloud (AWS), which return a manifest file.

Adaptive Bitrate Selection: Using the manifest, the client determines the optimal CDN node and requests chunks at the highest bitrate the user's connection can sustain.

Dynamic Failover: If the selected path experiences network congestion, the client dynamically downgrades the bitrate or switches to an alternate nearby Open Connect node.

Slide 15:

 Content Distribution Networks (CDNs) — Over-The-Top (OTT) Delivery

This slide explains the OTT delivery model and the fundamental management decisions CDN operators must solve.

What is OTT ("Over The Top")?

OTT describes services like Netflix, YouTube, or Hulu that deliver streaming media directly over the public Internet without managing or owning the underlying network infrastructure.

The Internet operates merely as a dumb, best-effort host-to-host transport pipeline ("Internet host-host communication as a service").

Key OTT CDN Challenges (Managing from the Edge):

Because OTT providers have no direct control over core Internet router routing or ISP line congestion, they must make two major algorithmic decisions:

Placement Policy: What specific video titles/chunks should be pushed to which CDN edge nodes ahead of time? (e.g., placing trending regional movies in local servers while keeping rare titles in centralized storage).

Server Selection & Adaptation Policy: Which CDN node should a particular user connect to in real time, and at what encoding bitrate?
